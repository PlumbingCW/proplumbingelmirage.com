Water Heater Replacement in El Mirage, AZ

Water heater replacement in El Mirage, AZ is the right call when a tank leaks from its body or has failed too many times to trust. A tank that has split cannot be patched, and standing water can ruin flooring and drywall quickly. The 24/7 emergency line takes calls at any hour, and same-day appointments are offered when available.

While you wait, close the cold supply valve above the tank and switch off the heater's power or gas control. That stops the flow and keeps the situation from getting worse. Then you can decide calmly what to buy.

Not every dead heater needs replacing. A bad element or thermostat is often a smaller job, covered on the water heater repair page. This page helps you tell the difference and plan the swap.

A failed tank: what to do in the first ten minutes

A tank that is leaking from the bottom or the side, or one that has dumped water onto the floor, is the textbook case for replacement. Water that gets under cabinets or flooring should be dealt with fast, so shutting off the supply comes first.

If you ever smell gas near the heater, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 before you call anyone else.

  • Turn the cold water shutoff on the line above the tank fully closed.
  • Cut power at the breaker for an electric unit, or set the gas control to off if there is no gas smell.
  • Take a photo of the leak and the data plate for your records and for the plumber.
  • Mop up what you safely can and keep people away from wet floors near outlets.
  • Call the 24/7 line and tell the plumber what fuel the heater uses.

Signs the tank is done, in plain terms

A tank rarely fails without warning. The common signs are moisture around the base, rust-colored hot water, popping noises, and a rim of mineral crust around fittings. A puddle that comes back after you wipe it up is the clearest signal.

Age is the other guide. The data plate on the side of the tank carries the manufacture date in its serial number. Ask the plumber to help you read it, since the code format differs by brand.

Water chemistry plays a part. The city's 2024 water quality report lists hardness from 4 to 9 grains per gallon with an average of 7, and scale from water like that typically builds on the tank floor and heating parts. Scale does not make every tank fail early, but it makes a neglected tank less forgiving.

If you see a wet patch away from the heater, rule out other sources before you buy anything. A leak detection visit can separate a heater leak from a supply line leak.

How a replacement day runs

The plumber can describe the plan for your home before starting. This is the usual sequence.

  1. Shut down and drain

    Water, power, and gas are isolated, and the old tank is drained and disconnected.

  2. Remove and inspect connections

    The plumber checks the shutoff valve, the supply lines, the vent, and the drain pan area for wear that should be fixed during the swap.

  3. Set the new tank

    The new unit is placed, leveled, and connected to water. A gas unit also gets its gas connection and vent tied in.

  4. Check safety devices

    The relief valve and its discharge pipe are reviewed. Ask the plumber whether your home needs an expansion tank.

  5. Fill, light, and test

    The tank is filled before any power or fire. Then the plumber confirms heating, vent draw on gas units, and leak-free joints.

Choosing what goes in

The best replacement is usually the same fuel in a similar size, because the gas line, vent, or circuit is already there. Switching types is possible, but it adds scope.

Same-type tank

Fastest and simplest. The existing connections, vent, and circuit are reused, so the job stays small.

Bigger or smaller tank

Size should match how many people use hot water at once. A tank that is too small runs out. One that is too large wastes energy.

Tankless

Saves floor space and heats on demand, but changes the gas line, venting, and maintenance. See the tankless water heater page for the trade-offs.

Heat pump hybrid

Uses less electricity, needs space for airflow, and has more parts. Ask the plumber about clearance and condensate drainage.

Gas or electric: what changes if you switch

Staying with your current fuel is easiest. If you are considering a switch, understand what each direction involves.

Gas to electric

  • Needs a dedicated circuit that may not exist at the heater location.
  • The old gas line must be capped safely.
  • The old vent may need to be sealed or removed.
  • Ask the El Mirage building department about permits for this change.

Electric to gas

  • Needs a gas supply at the heater location, which may mean new pipe.
  • Needs a proper vent to the outdoors.
  • Underground gas piping falls under the city's local burial rules, so ask about depth for any new run.
  • Gas work is the case where any smell means leave first and call the gas utility or 911.

Before the plumber arrives

  • Find the data plate and photograph the model number and manufacture date.
  • Measure the space the heater sits in, including door width.
  • Note any extra items attached, such as a recirculation pump or a water softener nearby.
  • Decide whether you want the same fuel, so you can answer the plumber's first question.
  • Move stored items out of the area so the old tank can come out.

What makes one replacement cost more than another

Two houses with the same heater model can get different prices. Here is what drives the difference.

Tank type and sizeLarger tanks and special models cost more than a standard unit.
Fuel changeSwitching from gas to electric or the reverse adds wiring, piping, or venting work.
Code itemsRelief valve piping, venting, and expansion control may need updating to meet the code in force.
Access and haul-awayTight closets, attics, and stairs make the old tank harder to remove.
Water damageA flooded floor or wall adds cleanup and repair that sits outside the heater price.

Homeowners insurance often covers sudden and accidental water damage from a burst tank but usually not the heater itself when it simply wears out. Read your policy and talk to your agent first.
