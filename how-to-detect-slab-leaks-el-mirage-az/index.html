How to Detect a Slab Leak in El Mirage, AZ: Signs and Checks

A slab leak hides under the floor, but it leaves clues you can read with your eyes, your feet and your water meter.

The short answer

A slab leak is a leak in a water pipe that runs under or inside a concrete slab foundation. You usually cannot see it, so you detect it by its side effects. Look for a water meter that keeps moving when every fixture is off. Look for a bill that climbs with no change in habits, a floor that feels warm in one spot, running water you can hear in a quiet house, or damp flooring and baseboards.

The most reliable home check is the meter test. Turn off everything that uses water, read the meter, wait, and read it again. If the numbers moved, water is escaping somewhere, and a plumber can then work out whether it is under the slab or elsewhere.

What does a slab leak look like from inside the house?

Most people never see the pipe. They notice one of these signs first, and often two or three together.

Warm or hot spot on the floor

A leaking hot line can heat the concrete above it. Bare feet find it before anything else does. Tile and wood floors both pass the warmth through.

Water sound with nothing running

A hiss or trickle at night, when the house is silent, can mean pressurized water is escaping from a pipe. Put your ear near the floor or the base of a wall.

Damp carpet, lifting tile or swollen baseboard

Water finds the weakest path up through slab cracks and wall bases. Flooring that cups, buckles or smells musty needs a closer look.

A higher bill with no new habits

Slow leaks run all day and night. The cost shows up on the statement long before anything shows on the floor.

A meter that moves when you are not using water

This is the clearest sign of any hidden leak. It does not tell you where the leak is, only that one exists.

Low hot water pressure or a heater that runs more

If the leak is on the hot side, the heater keeps refilling and reheating. You may notice weaker flow at the taps.

Step by step: the meter test you can do in ten minutes

This test separates a hidden leak from normal use. Do it when nobody is home or everyone has agreed to stop using water.

  1. Shut off every water use

    Turn off taps, dishwasher, washing machine and ice maker. Check that no toilet is refilling and no sprinkler zone is running.

  2. Find the meter box

    It is typically in a ground-level box near the street or property line. Lift the lid carefully, since insects and spiders like the shade, and use a screwdriver or a gloved hand.

  3. Look at the leak indicator

    Many meters have a small dial or triangle that spins when any water moves. If it turns with everything off, you already have your answer.

  4. Write down the read

    Note every digit on the register. Take a phone photo so you have a record with the time.

  5. Wait and read again

    Give it at least 30 minutes with no water use, or a few hours if you can. A change in the numbers means water is leaving the system.

  6. Close the home shutoff and recheck

    If the house main is closed and the meter stops, the leak is inside the house side. If it keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and the house, often an underground line.

Quick checks that rule out the cheap culprits

Many suspected slab leaks turn out to be something simpler. Spend a few minutes on these before you assume the worst.

  • Drop a few drops of food coloring in each toilet tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means a leaking flapper.
  • Look at the water heater base and the pressure relief discharge pipe for drips or mineral crust.
  • Check hose bibs, irrigation valves and the sprinkler controller. A stuck zone valve can keep water flowing all day.
  • Listen at each sink, tub and washing machine box for a faint hiss.
  • Feel along the floor in each room, slowly, with bare feet or the back of your hand.
  • Check the ceiling below any upstairs bathroom if you have a two-story home, since that is a different leak path.

Can you pinpoint it yourself, and where does DIY stop?

You can confirm that a leak exists. Finding the exact spot under concrete is another matter.

What you can do at home

  • Read the meter and run the shutoff test.
  • Check toilets, heater, hose bibs and irrigation.
  • Note warm spots, damp areas and sounds, with dates.
  • Close the main valve if water is actively damaging the home.
  • Photograph the meter and any damage for your records.

What needs a plumber

  • Locating the leak to a few feet using listening gear and thermal imaging.
  • Telling a hot line from a cold line leak.
  • Deciding between repair, reroute or replacing the line.
  • Opening flooring or concrete only where needed.
  • Pressure testing sections of pipe to isolate the failure.

What the plumber does to find a slab leak

A plumber typically starts by confirming the symptoms and closing off parts of the system to narrow the search. Pressure tests on the hot and cold lines show which side is losing water.

Electronic listening equipment picks up the sound of water escaping, and thermal imaging can show warm paths on the floor. Leak detection methods like these reduce the amount of flooring or concrete that has to be opened. They are not perfect, and a very small leak or a noisy pipe can make a result less certain.

Once the leak is located, the options usually are a spot repair, rerouting the line through walls or attic, or repiping if several lines are failing. The right choice depends on pipe condition and access. Ask the plumber to explain why they recommend one option over another.

Should you wait and watch, or act now?

Act now if the meter is moving with everything off. A leak that runs for weeks can undermine flooring, raise your bill and soften the soil near the foundation.

Close the main valve if you hear water or see it spreading. If a ceiling, outlet or appliance is wet, stay clear and call the 24/7 line. For a confirmed leak under the floor, our page on slab leak repair explains the repair options in more detail.

What El Mirage homeowners should know before testing

Census-based data puts the median El Mirage home at about 2004, and most homes date from the 2000s boom. That tells you many homes are newer tract builds, but it does not tell you the foundation type or pipe material of your house. Ask the plumber to confirm what your home actually has before you assume a slab leak.

The city's 2024 water quality report lists groundwater hardness of 4 to 9 grains per gallon, with an average of 7. Mineral scale from hard water can clog small fittings and valves, so a fixture that drips is more often a worn part than a slab problem. Rule that out first.

If the meter test points to a leak and your bill looks off, contact city customer service. Ask about the read and about any leak adjustment, since the city's water utility is run by its Public Works department.
