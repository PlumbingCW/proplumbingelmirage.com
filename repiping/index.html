Repiping in El Mirage, AZ

Repiping in El Mirage, AZ means replacing the supply pipes inside your house, the lines that bring water to every tap, with new pipe. It is a big job, and it is not the right answer for every leak. This page explains when it pays off and when a simple repair is the better choice.

Many homeowners call after the second or third pinhole leak in a year. That pattern is the main reason people consider a repipe. A single leak on an otherwise sound line is usually just a repair.

Census-based data puts the median El Mirage home at about 2004. Pipe material for any one house is not known without looking, so the plumber should confirm what is in your walls before suggesting anything.

Repair or repipe: how to decide

The choice is about pattern, not just the latest leak.

A repair is usually enough when

  • One leak, with sound pipe on both sides of the cut.
  • The leak is at a fitting or a failed valve.
  • The pipe is accessible without opening many walls.
  • No other signs of wear show up.

Repiping starts to make sense when

  • Leaks keep returning in new places.
  • The plumber finds thin, scaled or brittle pipe.
  • Water quality or flow is poor at every tap.
  • A known problem pipe type runs through the house.

Galvanized pipe and copper: what each means here

Galvanized steel pipe rusts from the inside, narrows over time and is the classic reason for repiping. It is more common in much older homes. Because most El Mirage houses date from the 2000s, finding galvanized pipe would be unusual, though a few older homes and additions exist.

Copper is a good pipe that can still develop pinholes, especially where water is aggressive or flow is fast. If you have a copper leak, a burst pipe repair may be all you need.

The city's 2024 water quality report lists groundwater from nine city wells with hardness from 4 to 9 grains per gallon. Mineral scale can build inside pipes and fittings over many years. Whether that has affected yours is something only a look at the pipe will answer.

How a repipe job works

Details change with the house. This is the usual sequence, and the plumber will explain yours.

  1. Inspect and confirm the material

    The plumber looks at accessible pipe, checks fixtures and may cut a small test section to see its condition.

  2. Plan the route

    New lines are routed through walls, attic or crawl areas. The plumber decides what has to be opened.

  3. Check the permit question

    Ask the El Mirage building department whether your job needs a permit. The plumber should tell you who pulls it.

  4. Shut off and swap

    Water is turned off while old lines are removed and new ones joined. Plan to be without water for part of the work.

  5. Test under pressure

    The new system is pressurized and checked at every joint before walls are closed.

  6. Patch and finish

    Drywall openings are patched. Paint and texture may be separate work, so ask who does it.

When repiping is not worth it

A repipe is not a fix for a single leak, a clogged aerator or low pressure from a failing regulator. Check those first, because they cost far less. When the source of a leak is unclear, leak detection comes before any repipe talk.

It is also a poor value when you plan to sell soon and the pipes are sound. A buyer's inspector looks at condition, not age. A clean report from a plumber may be worth more than a repipe done in a rush.

If the plumber cannot tell you which pipe is failing, ask for a camera or pressure test, or a look at a cut section. A quote that arrives before anyone has inspected a pipe is a guess.

Questions to ask before you sign

Use these to compare any repipe quote you receive.

  • What pipe do I have now, and what will you install?
  • Is the full house being repiped, or only part of it?
  • How many walls and ceilings will be opened, and who patches them?
  • Does the job need a permit, and who handles it?
  • How long will the water be off in a typical day of work?
  • Is the plumber's Arizona ROC license current? Check Contractor Search on the ROC site.
  • What happens to the old pipe, and what is covered if a joint leaks later?

Water heater, valves and fixtures at the same time

When walls are open, some work is cheaper to do at the same time than later.

Shutoff valves

Old fixture valves that stick or drip can be replaced while the lines are open.

Water heater connections

If your heater is near the end of its life, talk through water heater replacement before the new pipe goes in.

Pressure regulator

A failed regulator can stress new pipe, so ask the plumber to check it.

Hose bibs

Leaky outdoor spigots are easy to fix when the nearby lines are open.

Mixed pipe and partial repipes

A partial repipe replaces only the bad run, for example the hot water line or one bathroom. It can be the sensible middle path when most of the house is sound.

The trade-off is that the older pipe stays in the walls, and it may leak later. Ask the plumber how the new section connects and what condition the old pipe is in.
