Tankless Water Heater in El Mirage, AZ

A tankless water heater in El Mirage, AZ heats water as it flows instead of storing a tank of it. That means no standby tank and no running out mid-shower, but it also means a heat exchanger that sees every drop of hard water. The city's 2024 water quality report lists hardness from 4 to 9 grains per gallon with an average of 7.

That number is the reason descaling comes up on almost every tankless conversation here. Mineral scale coats the narrow passages inside the unit, and it builds quietly until the heater slows down, throws an error code, or shuts off.

This page covers whether a tankless unit fits your home, what a flush does and does not fix, and how it compares with a tank. If you are weighing a swap, see the water heater installation page for the planning steps.

Is tankless right for your house?

Tankless is a good fit in some homes and a poor one in others. Here is the honest picture.

Where tankless works well

  • Households that want to free floor space.
  • Homes where long hot showers back to back are common.
  • Owners who will schedule regular flushing.
  • Houses where the gas line and vent can be upgraded at reasonable effort.

Where it falls short

  • A single unit can struggle if several taps run hot water at once.
  • Upfront cost is usually higher than a tank, plus any gas and vent work.
  • Hard water scale can cut efficiency and shorten life if ignored.
  • A short wait for hot water at distant taps does not go away.

Descaling and flushing, step by step

A flush circulates a mild acid solution, often vinegar or a manufacturer-approved descaler, through the heat exchanger. Many units have service valves for this. Ask the plumber to do it, or follow your manual if you do it yourself.

  1. Turn off power and gas

    Shut off the unit and its gas supply, and close the water isolation valves.

  2. Connect the flush hoses

    Hoses link a pump and bucket to the service ports so the solution can circulate.

  3. Circulate the solution

    The descaler runs through for the time the manufacturer suggests, loosening mineral buildup.

  4. Rinse and clean the screen

    Fresh water flushes out the solution and loosened scale. The inlet screen is cleaned.

  5. Restore and test

    Valves are reopened, the unit is restarted, and the plumber checks flow, temperature, and any code.

How often should you flush in 7 gpg water?

No single schedule fits every home. Manufacturers publish their own intervals, and many suggest at least yearly in hard water areas. With the city's reported average of 7 grains per gallon, a yearly flush is a reasonable starting point, and some owners go more often.

How often depends on how much hot water you use, the unit's setting, and whether a softener is in place. Ask the plumber how heavy the scale looks when they open the unit and set the next interval from that.

Treating the water before it reaches the heater cuts scale at the source. See water softener and filtration for what that involves and what the equipment cannot do.

What a flush cannot fix

Flushing removes loose scale. It cannot undo a cracked heat exchanger, a failed flow sensor, or a unit that has run with heavy buildup for years. If the unit was never flushed, the first service may reveal damage that was hiding under the scale.

A plumber should tell you plainly if the unit is past saving. A repair that costs most of a replacement is a poor trade.

Gas, venting, and sizing basics

Gas tankless units draw a lot of fuel quickly. Many homes need a larger gas line or a different meter setting than a tank used. Venting is also different, often with special pipe. Ask the plumber to check both before you buy.

If you smell gas at any time, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 before anyone calls a plumber.

Sizing depends on how many fixtures run together and how cold the incoming water is. A unit that is too small gives lukewarm water when demand rises. Ask the plumber to size it from your actual use, not from the box.

When tankless repair is worth it

Repair depends on what failed and how old the unit is.

Inlet screen or flow sensor

Cheap to service and often the cause of cutting out. Usually worth fixing.

Igniter or gas valve

Worth repairing on a newer unit. On an old unit, weigh the part price against replacement.

Scaled heat exchanger

A flush may clear it. If scale has already damaged the exchanger, replacement is the honest answer.

Repeated codes

If the same code returns after service, ask whether a deeper fault or old age is behind it.

Before you book a service visit

  • Write down the model number and any error code showing on the display.
  • Note when the problem happens, such as lukewarm water at full flow or cutting out.
  • Find your manual or the manufacturer's service interval.
  • Check whether service valves are installed on the water lines.
  • Tell the plumber if you have a softener or conditioner on the water line.

If a tank is the better answer

Some households are better served by a conventional tank, especially if the budget is tight or the gas line cannot be upgraded. If you have a failed tank and are choosing between types, see water heater replacement for the repair-versus-replace decision.
